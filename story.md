# Attending

The agency called it *attendance*, which made it sound like a school register. Marisol called it what it was, but only to herself, and only on the drive home.

She got the call at 6:40 on a Tuesday: Mr. Alvin Poe, 81, no next of kin listed, third floor of a walk-up on Halsey Street. The dispatcher was Rene, whose voice came through the phone like a receipt printing. "Hospice was out this morning," Rene said. "They're not due back till Thursday. The neighbor has a key. She's the one who called us."

"How long does he have."

"Nobody says. That's why we're going."

Marisol packed the bag the way she always packed it. Phone charger. A paperback with a broken spine. Thermos of tea. The small battery radio. A card with the after-hours number. The kit — gloves, wipes, a plastic bag, a form that nobody ever signed. She had started bringing the radio after a man in East Liberty spent his last hours listening to a refrigerator hum, because there was nothing else in the room to listen to.

She parked on Halsey under a streetlight that flickered. The building had a buzzer panel with the names typed on strips of tape. Poe, 3B. The lockbox hung off the railing where Rene said it would, and the code was his birthday, which someone at the agency thought was respectful and which Marisol thought was a way to make a dying man's apartment as easy to open as a mailbox.

Three flights. Onions and old carpet. On the landing a woman in a housedress stood with her arms crossed, keys in her fist.

"You're the one they send," she said. Not a question.

"Marisol."

"Adaeze. I'm across the hall." She didn't move. "He doesn't like the overhead light. There's a lamp. And the radio's on the ballgame. He likes the ballgame, but he's been out of it since yesterday, so it doesn't matter what he likes."

"It matters."

Adaeze looked at her a second, then handed over the keys.

The apartment was small and very clean. That was the first thing — the dishes put away, the counters wiped, a folded blanket on the arm of the couch. The second thing was the bed, a hospital bed pushed into the living room because the bedroom was too small to turn a body in, and in it Mr. Alvin Poe, 81, small under the sheet, mouth open, breathing like a man walking uphill.

The radio was on low. A game. Marisol turned it up one notch and turned off the overhead light. The lamp had a shade the color of weak tea, and it made the room smaller and warmer than it was. On the shelf above the bed: a row of National Geographics, a wooden duck, a photograph of a woman in a wide hat, a coffee can full of pens. On the fridge, held by a magnet shaped like Ohio, a phone number in blue ballpoint. Denise, with an area code she didn't know.

She sat in the chair that had been pulled up beside the bed. She read a page of the paperback and didn't turn it. She watched his chest.

Around eight, Adaeze knocked once and came in with a plate under foil. "I made too much," she said, which was a lie she had decided on. She set the plate on the counter and sat on the couch, not close, and looked at Mr. Poe the way you look at weather.

"Six years I've had that key," she said. "His wife, before. She left it with me. Then she passed, and he never asked for it back."

Marisol didn't answer. It was usually better not to.

"He's got a daughter. Denise. Tucson. She calls Sundays, mostly. He puts her on speaker and they talk about the weather in two places."

"Does she know?"

"I called her this morning. She said she'd come." Adaeze smoothed the housedress over her knees. "People say a lot of things in the morning."

They sat. The ballgame ended. The radio played a commercial for a car dealership, then the news, then music, and neither of them reached over to change it.

At nine the landline rang — a heavy plastic phone on the side table — and Marisol answered it, because it was ringing and there was nobody else in the apartment who could.

"Who is this?" A woman's voice, thin.

"This is Marisol. I'm with your father."

A pause. "Is he — "

"He's still here."

A longer pause. "I'm coming Thursday. I can't get a flight before Thursday."

"He might not last till Thursday."

The line made a sound like wind. "I know," Denise said. Then: "Is he awake?"

"No."

"Can you — " She stopped. Started again. "Can you put the phone by him. Just for a minute."

Marisol carried the phone to the bed and set the receiver against his ear, careful, and stood holding the cord so it wouldn't pull. Denise talked. Marisol couldn't hear the words, only the shape of them, rising and falling. Mr. Poe's breathing went on the way it had been going on. His hand lay on the sheet and it didn't move.

After a while Denise said thank you and hung up, and Marisol put the phone back on the table.

Adaeze had gone quiet. Then she said, "You do this a lot?"

"Enough."

"How do you stand it."

Marisol thought about the word she had for it. She didn't say it in apartments. "I stand it," she said.

Adaeze nodded like that was an answer. At half past ten she took her plate, still covered, and went back across the hall, and the apartment was just the two of them and the radio.

Marisol knew the sound when it came, the way the space between breaths opened and kept opening. She put the book down. She turned the radio off, then turned it back on, lower, and didn't know why she did that either. She stayed in the chair with her hands in her lap.

At 11:20 he stopped. She waited. She put two fingers to his wrist, then to his neck, and held them there longer than she needed to, then looked at the clock and wrote the time on the form and signed it and folded it into her bag.

She called Rene. "Poe," she said. "Eleven twenty."

"Okay," Rene said. "I'll call the funeral home. You know the number."

"I know it."

"Go home, Marisol."

She opened a window a crack, the way the agency liked, though nobody had ever told her why. Adaeze was at her door before Marisol knocked, as if she had been standing behind it.

"He's gone," Marisol said.

Adaeze nodded slowly, then went in and stood by the bed with her hands folded. She didn't cry. She looked at the photograph of the woman in the wide hat for a while.

"He kept it clean," she said. "After she passed. Every day, the dishes."

"I noticed."

Marisol gathered her things. She left the plate of food on the counter, because it wasn't hers to take and wasn't hers to throw away. At the door Adaeze said, "Will you come back? For the — " and gestured vaguely at the apartment, the arrangements, the things.

"No," Marisol said. "Someone else comes for that."

"That's the job," Adaeze said.

"That's the job."

The funeral home would be forty minutes. Waiting wasn't her part. She took the stairs. Outside, the streetlight was still flickering, and the air had the wet cold that comes off the river at night.

She drove. The bridge was empty and she had the radio off.

The agency called it attendance. Rene called it a shift. The forms called it a presence call, which was the worst of them. None of it was wrong, exactly, and none of it was the thing.

On the bridge, with nobody in the car to hear it, Marisol said the word out loud, the way she only ever said it here.

*Deathwatch.*

It was an ugly word and the right size. Attendance fit on a form; this fit nowhere.

The light at the end of the bridge turned green, and she took her foot off the brake.
